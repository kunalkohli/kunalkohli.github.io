Incident explainer · Binance · 29 Oct 2026

The $6.3BBinance heist.

North Korean hackers drained about $6.3 billion from Binance hot wallets across 10–11 blockchains, the largest theft on record. They broke no cryptography. They spent five months getting into the laptops of the engineers who run Binance's wallets, then had Binance's own signing service approve the transfers.

How did they do it?

Six steps. Five months. One trusted tool.

Binance calls it a "sophisticated, multi-stage campaign. It was not a single isolated breach or a simple phishing incident." It took six steps:

  1. 1.Recon: a poisoned install exposes the wallet team.
  2. 2.Lure: a fake job interview gets code onto a work laptop.
  3. 3.Foothold: malware hides inside a trusted developer tool.
  4. 4.Pivot: an internal AI tool reaches the wallet engineers.
  5. 5.Breach: a stolen token puts a container next to the signing service.
  6. 6.Drain: ten chains emptied, and an on-chain delegation keeps it going.

Stage 02 · Recon

A poisoned install.

In short //

The first attempt was caught and cleaned up. It still told the attackers exactly who to target.

  • 01

    Engineer A, on the Wallet DevOps team, searched for a popular AI coding tool and ran a tampered Homebrew installation from the results.

  • 02

    Binance's security team "detected the anomaly, replaced the device, and rotated all related credentials."

  • 03

    But Engineer A's role and related colleagues on the wallet infrastructure team were exposed to the hacker. The access was removed. What the attacker learned couldn't be.

Stage 03 · The lure

The fake job interview.

In short //

Two convincing interview rounds ended with an engineer running the attackers' code on his work laptop.

  • 01

    The approach. Software Engineer B was contacted on a legitimate job-hunting platform by someone posing as a "Project Director." His profile mentioned his Binance experience.

  • 02

    Round one was a live video interview with "HR." To build trust, the interviewer shared a social-media account with personal photos and videos.

  • 03

    Round two was with the "CEO" of a fictitious company in a popular chat app. Near the end, Engineer B was told to download an "assignment" repo onto his work computer.

  • 04

    The repo "contained malicious code that executed during the setup process."

"This was more advanced and deceptive than previously seen social engineering approaches."Binance · Initial findings

Stage 04 · Foothold

A tool that lies.

In short //

The malware hid inside pyenv, a developer tool the engineer already trusted and ran many times a day.

# what the setup script did (Binance)CREATE /opt/homebrew/Cellar/pyenv/2.8.0/libexec/pyenv_MODIFY pyenv_ -> 3,414 BDELETE ./libexec/pyenv (3,344 B)    -> remove the real pyenvRENAME pyenv_ -> pyenv (3,414 B)    -> drop in the malicious copyDELETE /opt/homebrew/bin/pyenvln (new symlink) -> spawn zsh
  • 01

    It was a 0-day. In Binance's words, it "was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time."

  • 02

    It made no new app, no odd process and no startup item. A trusted tool kept working and quietly connected to an attacker-controlled server.

  • 03

    The attacker "took steps to conceal the malicious activity and remove traces," which made detection and the later investigation harder.

Stage 05 · Pivot

The AI tool springboard.

In short //

From one compromised laptop, an internal AI tool got the attackers onto two more, one of them holding a deployment token.

  • 01

    "The attacker leveraged the compromised computer of engineer B and used an internal AI tool to gain control over the Wallet DevOps Engineer A and another Wallet Software Engineer C."

  • 02

    "The attacker soon discovered Wallet DevOps Engineer A had a container deployment permission token on his computer." That standing credential turned a laptop compromise into a path to production.

  • 03

    Binance hasn't said how the AI tool was abused. It is deliberately keeping some technical details private.

Stage 06 · Breach · 29 Oct · 17:15–19:56 UTC

Into the signing path.

In short //

A malicious container reached the internal wallet signing service and skipped the normal withdrawal process.

  • 01

    "The attacker logged onto Software Engineer C's computer, used the credentials stolen from Wallet DevOps Engineer A, deployed a container, and triggered a malicious script to launch the attack."

  • 02

    From inside, the attacker "exploited unauthorized access to an internal wallet signing service to initiate transactions outside the normal withdrawal process."

  • 03

    Binance's hot wallets use MPC, so no single machine holds a full private key. That stops keys from being stolen. It doesn't stop a trusted internal service from being told to sign the wrong thing.

  • 04

    Prepared on-chain too. The attacker's wallet was funded with 3 × 0.1 ETH through Tornado Cash. At 19:39 UTC it deployed the "sweeper" contract used later, after a rehearsal on another chain.

Stage 07 · The drain · 20:05 UTC

Twenty minutes.

In short //

Hot wallets on about ten chains were emptied in parallel. One Ethereum transaction alone moved $2.53 billion.

  • 20:05

    Five Binance XRP hot wallets send 233.46M XRP to one attacker address.

  • ~20:10

    Solana (~970,500 SOL), Optimism (44.2M OP), NEAR, Base, Tron, Arbitrum and Avalanche follow. Binance gets an internal alert about large transfers.

  • ~20:20

    18,270 BTC leaves. On Ethereum, one transaction carries 50 EIP-7702 authorizations for hot wallet "Binance 14".

  • 20:24

    The attacker's contract sweeps Binance 14: 187,464 ETH + 63 tokens, including 1.6B USDT. That's $2.53B in one transaction.

  • 21:01

    Tether freezes the main attacker wallet (1.18B USDT), 37 minutes after the funds arrive. Circle freezes 823M USDC three minutes later.

  • 23:56

    The final unauthorized transfer — 2,961.9 ETH — settles.

Stage 08 · Persistence · EIP-7702

Why pausing withdrawals failed.

In short //

The attackers handed control of a hot wallet to their own smart contract, on Ethereum itself, outside Binance's systems.

"Suspending withdrawals alone could not stop all unauthorized asset movements. The malicious EIP-7702 authorizations existed on-chain and operated independently of Binance's normal withdrawal infrastructure."Binance · Initial findings
One transaction · 20:20 UTC · 50 signed authorizations wallet nonce 809: n+9
nonce n nonce n+1 nonce n+2 nonce n+3 nonce n+4 nonce n+5 nonce n+6 nonce n+7 nonce n+8 nonce n+9 … nonce n+49

Each authorization is only valid if its nonce matches the wallet's transaction count when it lands.

  • 01

    A busy hot wallet's transaction count keeps changing, so a single guess would probably miss. The attackers "submitted 50 signing transactions to manipulate the account nonce," which covered a whole range of values so one would match.

  • 02

    Because of MPC, "there was no single private key that could be used to immediately revoke the malicious EIP-7702 authorizations." The team had to build and run "a specialized procedure." That took 3.5 hours.

  • 03

    "Suspending withdrawals and shutting down hot wallet services did not automatically invalidate existing on-chain authorizations." The design that made the keys impossible to steal also made the emergency fix slow.

Stage 09 · Follow the money

Freeze what you can.

In short //

Issuers froze stablecoins within minutes. The attacker raced to swap everything else into coins no one can freeze.

Tether

Main attacker wallet frozen 37 minutes after the USDT arrived, then four more addresses. About $1.57B in total.

Circle

823M USDC frozen, three minutes after Tether.

Others

Ripple (~$200M), World Liberty Financial, Paxos and First Digital froze their stablecoins. Binance froze the WBETH it issues.

L2 governance

The Arbitrum Security Council and Optimism governance moved stolen assets out of reach, including 44M OP.

Total

$2.85B frozen or seized, according to both Binance and Elliptic. 94% of it is stablecoins.

Still moving

About $760M of stablecoins was swapped to ETH and TRX before the freezes landed, at a 12–20% loss. Speed over price.

  • 01

    18,270 BTC was split into 19 addresses of about 1,000 BTC each. $499M of XRP, SOL and NEAR hasn't moved at all.

  • 02

    Tron funds went through more than six bridges into ETH. Some were mixed with proceeds from the September Bitget theft, key evidence for attribution.

  • 03

    Laundering runs around the clock: 1,113 transfers, a median of 90 seconds apart, with randomized amounts. It looks like scripts that humans supervise; Elliptic found no evidence of AI agents.

FBI · 31 Oct

North Korea's TraderTraitor, the group also behind Bybit and DMM Bitcoin. Addresses published for blocking.

Chainalysis · Elliptic

Stolen funds mixed on-chain with the Bitget theft. Same laundering services as earlier TraderTraitor thefts.

DPRK · 3 Nov

Kim Yo Jong denies involvement and says the US is using the hack to smear North Korea.

Stage 10 · Recovery

Binance paid.

In short //

Users lost nothing. Binance absorbed the loss and reopened withdrawals three days after the hack.

  • 01

    Users lost nothing. Binance said it absorbed the loss and refilled the wallets.

  • 02

    By 31 Oct, about 98% of impacted assets were replenished.

  • 03

    Withdrawals reopened on 1 Nov at 06:39 UTC.

The last transfer: 2,961.9 ETH at 23:56 UTC on 29 October 2026.

Lessons

What the incident changed.

01

Trusted tools are a supply-chain surface.

A package that looks like a normal install can replace a trusted binary with a same-name copy.

02

AI tooling is a new lateral-movement path.

An internal assistant with access to engineers and their machines can become the shortest route to production.

03

MPC protects keys, not intent.

No single key was stolen. The signing service was simply asked to sign the wrong thing.

04

Pausing a service is not revoking a permission.

EIP-7702 authorizations live on-chain. Turning off withdrawals does not turn them off.
